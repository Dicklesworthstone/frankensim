A drum, a splash cymbal and a concert grand, computed from geometry, materials and contact. Strings get their partials from tension, stiffness and mass. Heads and soundboards get their modes from finite elements. A felt hammer or a stick collides with them, and the air carries the result to a microphone placed at a point in space. Every mechanical step closes an energy ledger, or the render stops.

Two ways to hear it: offline reference renders that took up to 41 minutes per clip, and a live engine compiled to WebAssembly that you can play in this tab. The only recordings on this page are the real instruments we grade ourselves against, and they are clearly marked.

Offline reference

A 14×6½-inch drum, from its description.

Two films of PET over a sealed air column, a wooden stick with an integrated radius profile, 768,000 mechanical steps per second, and a boundary-element model of the whole closed drum carrying sound to two microphones 27 cm above the batter. Each comparison below changes exactly one physical thing.

Hit it harder: the pitch glide is geometry

A tom drops in pitch after a hard hit because a deflected membrane is stretched, which raises its tension for a moment. The linear twin has the same mass, stiffness and air but leaves out that one term in the potential energy. Harder only makes it louder. The stretching drum starts sharp and glides down. The plot tracks the measured fundamental of each rendered clip.

240 contacts, not a noise layer

Twenty steel strands, each with eight modes and twelve contact points against the resonant head, are solved together with the drum. "Wires off" is the same equations with a 3 mm gap instead of 20 µm. With the wires engaged, energy above 1 kHz rises from −21.8 dB to −4.6 dB of the total. That buzz comes from collisions; the head modes themselves stop at 500 Hz.

Where you strike decides which modes speak

At the centre the stick sits on the nodal diameters of every asymmetric mode, so the axisymmetric modes dominate. Moving toward the hoop wakes the (1,1) and (2,1) pairs: the measured spectral centroid of these renders rises from 230 Hz at the centre to 254 Hz at 75 mm and 610 Hz near the hoop.

An unevenly tensioned head beats

The varied head has a spatial prestress tensor (stiffer along one diameter) applied before the eigen-solve. Degenerate mode pairs split, and the split pairs beat. No oscillator was detuned; the stress field was changed.

A hard tip against a felt face

The same 2.5 m/s stroke through a Hertz tip and through a finite felt face with its own loading/unloading hysteresis and crush memory. We expected the felt to sound darker. Measured on these renders it is brighter: spectral centroid 224 Hz against 207 Hz, and 10 dB more energy above 1 kHz. Each of the mallet face's four contact sites keeps its own felt history and can catch the head's asymmetric modes separately. We report the measurement rather than the expectation, and have not yet traced which mechanism dominates.

Two hands, from a force score

Two sticks are driven by a hand-force program rather than triggered samples. Each stroke can land, rebound or miss, depending on where the head is at that instant.

Offline reference

An 8-inch splash: the low skeleton of a cymbal.

The shell is a tapered bronze dome meshed into 448 curved triangles, with nonlinear membrane strain and bending. It rests on three felt pads, and the radiating skin includes both faces and the 0.5 mm edge. The model keeps its 30 lowest elastic modes, up to 1.2 kHz, and its radiation filters are verified to 1.64 kHz. A real splash shimmers far above that, so what you hear is the part this model resolves. The dashed line on each spectrogram marks where verification ends. Content above it comes from contact impulses in the computed mechanics, passed through a filter that is unverified up there, and nothing was added to fill the gap.

Offline reference

A concert grand, all 234 strings at once.

The string scale is the published RT-0425 model of a Steinway D: 88 courses with lengths, linear densities, tensions and bending stiffness. The soundboard is a ribbed orthotropic spruce plate transcribed from published Model D drawings. Every string of every key stays in the simulation all the time, which is why the effects below need no "resonance layer".

The strings nobody struck

Hold C4, E4 and G4 down silently, so that their dampers lift without a hammer moving. Then strike C3 staccato and let it go. C3's dampers stop its own strings, but the shared bridge has already passed energy into the undamped C-major strings, and they keep singing. Compare it with the same strike alone, and with the pedal down, which lifts every damper. Use “Listen closer” to raise only the playback level of the quiet tail.

pp, mf, ff: the felt hardens

Hammer felt is stiffer the more it is squeezed, so a faster hammer stays in contact for less time. A shorter contact feeds energy into higher partials, which is why ff is brighter and not just louder. Beside the clips is the bridge velocity under the key: the force that actually reaches the soundboard.

Why piano octaves are wider than 2:1

A steel string is a little stiff, so its partials run sharp: fₙ = n·f₁·√(1+Bn²)/√(1+B). The effect grows up the keyboard. With exact 2:1 first partials, A5's second partial lands 5.5 Hz (5.4 cents) above A6. Stretch tuning moves them together by changing each string's tension, not by retuning an oscillator. The clips play A4+A5, then A5+A6 from 2.6 s. The plot zooms into the rendered sound near 1760 Hz, where the two tunings differ.

The bottom and the top

A0 is a single 2.01 m wound string with partials so stretched that the 20th lies more than a semitone sharp of 20·f₁. C6 has three short plain strings and a much shorter hammer contact.

Solo the soundboard

Exact modal sum

A2 at 2.5 m/s, split into the pressure that each loaded soundboard mode contributes at the microphone. Mute and solo the modes: the signed sum of every channel is the rendered sound to 10⁻¹⁴, including the cancellation between modes.

Graded against real instruments

How close is it to the real thing?

Sounding plausible is easy to fake, so we put FrankenSim's renders next to real recordings of the same notes, from the University of Iowa's free instrument samples: a Steinway & Sons Model B grand, and a splash cymbal recorded in an anechoic chamber. We measured the same things on both. The two headline gaps sit next to rulers made only of real instruments: how much the real piano changes between two dynamics, and how far one real cymbal is from another.

What the comparison shows

    Method: both signals are high-passed at 40–70 Hz to remove room rumble, and each pair is aligned at its note onset, measured against the recording's own noise floor. Partial frequencies come from a 1 s window, and B is fitted from (fₙ/n)² = f₀²(1+Bn²) over partials above the noise floor. Decay rates are linear fits of each partial over 0.2–2.6 s (0.2–2.0 s at C6). The Iowa piano was recorded with two microphones 8 inches above the bass and treble strings in a normal room; the bass-side channel is used, because averaging two spaced microphones can cancel partials. FrankenSim's piano is heard at one point 1 m above its board with no room, so some differences belong to the two listening positions rather than the instruments. The cymbals were recorded 5 feet away in an anechoic chamber; FrankenSim's cymbal is also heard at a far-field point with no room, so those listening conditions are similar. Playback gets the same high-pass and an early-loudness match, and nothing else. The analysis is tools/verisim.py.

    Why it sounds different: tracing each gap to what the model leaves out

    A score says how far apart two sounds are, not why. Here each difference is followed back along the chain hammer → string → bridge → soundboard → air → microphone. Some links can be checked inside the simulation, which records the bridge's motion as well as the sound. Then one physical mechanism is switched on at a time and the note is rendered again. A suspicion counts as confirmed only when that experiment moves the measured gap.

    Method and honesty

    What is computed, what is estimated, what is absent.

    These instruments are descriptions, not instrument code. A drum is a text file of radii, thicknesses, moduli and tensions, composed out of FrankenSim's general physics crates: plates and shells, modal solvers, contact, port-Hamiltonian integration, boundary elements. The same crates serve the project's thermal, structural and fluid work.
