A concert grand and a drum kit, computed from geometry, materials and contact. Strings get their partials from tension, stiffness and mass; the soundboard gets its modes from finite elements; drum heads and cymbals are vibrating plates on a fine grid. A felt hammer, a stick or a beater collides with them, and the air carries the result to a microphone placed at a point in space.

Two ways to hear it: offline reference renders that took up to 41 minutes per clip, and a live engine compiled to WebAssembly that you can play in this tab. The only recordings on this page are the real instruments we grade ourselves against, and they are clearly marked.

Offline reference

A drum kit, every piece from one solver.

Kick, snare, toms, hi-hat, crash and ride are one object here: a thin plate on a fine grid. A drum head is a film under tension, clamped at its rim; a cymbal is a bronze shell with a free edge that bends, stretches and trades energy between its vibrations (von Kármán coupling), which is what turns a strike into a wash. Stick, beater, snare wires and hi-hat rims meet the surfaces through contact laws; the air inside a drum couples its two heads. The sizes copy a real kit recorded with overhead microphones, and each piece is played next to its recording below.

Live · WebAssembly

Hit a drum that is being solved right now.

Tap anywhere on the head. The heads are prestressed films meshed and eigen-solved in this tab, and the stick is a mass with a Hertz contact law. Change the tension and the drum is rebuilt in milliseconds: new frequencies, because new eigenvalues came out of the solver. This live engine is the earlier modal model: with only its lowest modes and light damping it rings longer and purer than a real drum. The kit above is the realistic one.

A grand piano you can play — with its strings visible.

Each key launches a hysteretic felt hammer into stiff strings with the published Steinway scale's lengths, tensions and stiffness. Every string you play shares one soundboard. Press nearer the front of a key to strike harder.

Offline reference

A concert grand, all 234 strings at once.

The string scale is the published RT-0425 model of a Steinway D: 88 courses with lengths, linear densities, tensions and bending stiffness. The soundboard is a ribbed orthotropic spruce plate transcribed from published Model D drawings. Every string of every key stays in the simulation all the time, which is why the effects below need no "resonance layer".

The strings nobody struck

Hold C4, E4 and G4 down silently, so that their dampers lift without a hammer moving. Then strike C3 staccato and let it go. C3's dampers stop its own strings, but the shared bridge has already passed energy into the undamped C-major strings, and they keep singing. Compare it with the same strike alone, and with the pedal down, which lifts every damper. Use “Listen closer” to raise only the playback level of the quiet tail.

pp, mf, ff: the felt hardens

Hammer felt is stiffer the more it is squeezed, so a faster hammer stays in contact for less time. A shorter contact feeds energy into higher partials, which is why ff is brighter and not just louder. Beside the clips is the bridge velocity under the key: the force that actually reaches the soundboard.

Why piano octaves are wider than 2:1

A steel string is a little stiff, so its partials run sharp: fₙ = n·f₁·√(1+Bn²)/√(1+B). The effect grows up the keyboard. With exact 2:1 first partials, A5's second partial lands 5.5 Hz (5.4 cents) above A6. Stretch tuning moves them together by changing each string's tension, not by retuning an oscillator. The clips play A4+A5, then A5+A6 from 2.6 s. The plot zooms into the rendered sound near 1760 Hz, where the two tunings differ.

The bottom and the top

A0 is a single 2.01 m wound string with partials so stretched that the 20th lies more than a semitone sharp of 20·f₁. C6 has three short plain strings and a much shorter hammer contact.

Solo the soundboard

Exact modal sum

A2 at 2.5 m/s, split into the pressure that each loaded soundboard mode contributes at the microphone. Mute and solo the modes: the signed sum of every channel is the rendered sound to 10⁻¹⁴, including the cancellation between modes.

Graded against real instruments

How close is it to the real thing?

Sounding plausible is easy to fake, so we put FrankenSim's renders next to real recordings of the same notes, from the University of Iowa's free instrument samples: a Steinway & Sons Model B grand. We measured the same things on both, and the headline gap sits next to a ruler made only of the real piano: how much it changes between two dynamics. The drum kit is graded the same way, piece by piece, in its own section.

What the comparison shows

    Method: both signals are high-passed at 40–70 Hz to remove room rumble, and each pair is aligned at its note onset, measured against the recording's own noise floor. Partial frequencies come from a 1 s window, and B is fitted from (fₙ/n)² = f₀²(1+Bn²) over partials above the noise floor. Decay rates are linear fits of each partial over 0.2–2.6 s (0.2–2.0 s at C6). The Iowa piano was recorded with two microphones 8 inches above the bass and treble strings in a normal room; the bass-side channel is used, because averaging two spaced microphones can cancel partials. FrankenSim's piano is heard at one point 1 m above its board with no room, so some differences belong to the two listening positions rather than the instruments. Playback gets the same high-pass and an early-loudness match, and nothing else. The analysis is tools/verisim.py.

    Why it sounds different: tracing each gap to what the model leaves out

    A score says how far apart two sounds are, not why. Here each difference is followed back along the chain hammer → string → bridge → soundboard → air → microphone. Some links can be checked inside the simulation, which records the bridge's motion as well as the sound. Then one physical mechanism is switched on at a time and the note is rendered again. A suspicion counts as confirmed only when that experiment moves the measured gap.

    Method and honesty

    What is computed, what is estimated, what is absent.

    These instruments are descriptions, not instrument code. A drum is a short list of radii, thicknesses, moduli and tensions; the piano is composed out of FrankenSim's general physics crates (plates and shells, modal solvers, contact, port-Hamiltonian integration, boundary elements), and the kit out of one plate solver, fs-vkplate. The same crates serve the project's thermal, structural and fluid work.
