Why Simulate a Kitchen from First Principles

A floorplan is a coupled thermal and human machine.

Commercial kitchens draw 30 to 430 kW inside tight, stainless rooms where gas burners, exhaust hoods, walk-in evaporators, hot pans, and moving cooks all fight the same air and corridor space. Static CAD drawings check footprints, and spreadsheets sum nameplate ratings, yet the expensive buildout mistakes happen where those domains collide.

01 · HVAC & ELECTRIFICATION

Does switching to induction pay back in hood and cooling tonnage?

Open commercial gas ranges transfer roughly 42% of burner energy into a skillet and dump the other 58% into the room and exhaust canopy. Ferromagnetic induction couples at ~86%, cutting convective plume buoyancy and letting mechanical engineers shrink makeup-air and HVAC tonnage before ordering rooftop units.

02 · COLD CHAIN FAILURE

When do hot hotel pans push a walk-in past 41 °F?

Rolling 20 hot 4-inch or 6-inch pans straight off the line while staff open the cooler door creates a double load: slow conductive core cooling inside deep pans plus warm humid air rushing across the top lintel (Gosney-Olama exchange). Coil frost then cuts evaporator heat transfer right when the box needs peak capacity.

03 · QUALITY VS. SAFETY

How much vent opening keeps fried food crisp above 135 °F?

Seal an insulated delivery carrier tightly and food stays warm, but trapped steam drives crust moisture up until its Gordon-Taylor glass-transition temperature falls below food temperature and the crust turns soft. Open the vent too wide and core temperature crosses the 135 °F hot-hold floor early.

04 · ERGONOMIC CLEARANCE

Will a 38-inch line pinch taller cooks when oven doors open?

An aisle that passes code on paper can still force a 95th-percentile cook to twist past an open combi door or bend 26 degrees forward lifting a 10 kg stockpot. Checking P05, P50, and P95 swept capsules and lumbar torque directly on the real 3D geometry catches door conflicts before steel is fabricated.

Chapter 03 · Steam Condensation & Yeast Oven-Spring

The Cavity & the Crust.

When steam enters a hot combi cavity while dough surface temperature sits below the dew point, water condenses onto the loaf and releases 2.26 MJ/kg of latent heat. That condensation spikes effective heat transfer into the crumb while keeping the outer skin pliable so yeast gas expands the scored boule before starch gelatinization and Maillard browning lock the crust.
